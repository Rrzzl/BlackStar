## 0. Purpose

This document defines the world of Black Star — its history, factions, geography, cultures, languages, characters, and writing style. Every piece of dialogue, lore fragment, environmental storytelling beat, and faction action must be consistent with this bible.

When in doubt, the rule is: **the galaxy is broken, the past was worse, and anyone trying to fix it is either lying, dying, or both.**

---

## 1. Setting premise

The year is **3814** by the old Terran calendar. Four hundred and twelve years ago, the **Human Concordance** — a civilization spanning eight hundred worlds, united under a single banner for nearly a millennium — collapsed in a single twenty-seven-day cataclysm known as the **Unmaking**. Nobody alive knows what caused it. The historians who might have known are dust. The records are corrupted, scattered, or actively hidden. What's left is a galaxy of broken colonies, feral warlords, scavenger states, and ruins older than anyone remembers building.

The Concordance wasn't the first civilization in this corner of the galaxy. Long before humans, something called the **First Ones** (the name is a placeholder — nobody knows what they called themselves) built a network of structures across a thousand worlds. Their ruins pre-date humanity by at least a hundred thousand years. Their technology, in places where it still works, is indistinguishable from magic. Most voidrunners who make a living raiding ruins are digging through First One sites, not Concordance ones — the Concordance ruins are picked clean, the First One ruins are inexhaustible and lethal.

The galaxy Black Star depicts is post-apocalyptic science fiction without the aesthetic of post-apocalyptic science fiction. Things still work. Ships still fly. Stations still trade. People still fall in love, raise children, and die of old age. But everything is patchwork. Every working machine is a hand-me-down. Every functioning institution is a ghost of something larger. The galaxy feels like a cathedral half-collapsed, where people have set up tents in the transept and call it a city.

Dates are in **Post-Concordance Years (PCY)**. Year 0 is the start of the Unmaking. Current in-game year is **412 PCY**.

## 2.5 Species

The galaxy was human-settled, but not human-exclusive. The Concordance absorbed or allied with several sapient species over its millennium, and the Unmaking spared or created several more. Four species are playable at launch. Many more exist as NPCs.

### Humans

Baseline humanity. Descendants of Concordance citizens and their colonial predecessors. Make up ~70% of the galactic population. Every faction has human members; no faction is exclusively human. Humans are the cultural default — food, architecture, language, fashion — because the Concordance imposed its aesthetic everywhere for a thousand years.

**Mechanical bias:** +1 to all skills at creation (flexible, well-rounded).

**Cultural notes:** Ambitious, fractious, mourning. Every human alive is descended from a survivor, and everyone knows it.

### The Drellan

Tall, silver-skinned, long-lived humans genetically modified during a late-Concordance colonial program (the **Drell Project**) to survive low-gravity, high-radiation worlds. They grew into a distinct subspecies over ten generations: average height 7'2", lifespan ~200 years, slower metabolism, a memory architecture that makes them compulsive archivists. The Concordance considered them the **heirs of the next era**. The Unmaking killed most of them; survivors formed tight enclaves in the Core and are disproportionately represented in the Iron Concord's officer corps and the Archivists.

**Mechanical bias:** +10% XP gain. -5% rep recovery with enemies (Drellan hold grudges).

**Cultural notes:** Proud, patient, often insufferable. A Drellan will remember the grievances of their great-grandparents. When they finally laugh, it is quiet and sincere. When they finally forgive, it is final.

**Speech pattern:** Formal phrasing. Full words, rarely contractions. Second-language cadence even in their native tongue — an artifact of how they were taught.

### The Ksar

Short (4'10" average), sturdy, plated-skin humanoids from a world whose atmosphere killed anything unarmored. Not genetically human — their biology predates human arrival in the galaxy, and the question of how they got spacefaring is a historical puzzle the Archivists argue about. The Concordance met the Ksar in year -520 and negotiated a famously equitable treaty; the Ksar have been treaty-faithful allies ever since, which is more than can be said for any human faction.

**Mechanical bias:** +15 HP base. -5% movement speed.

**Cultural notes:** Nomadic, communal, matrilineal. A Ksar's "family" may include thirty relatives and two dozen oath-kin. They remember debts in multi-generational detail. A Ksar who owes you a favor will find your grandchildren to repay it.

**Speech pattern:** Plural pronouns ("we did this thing") even when speaking alone. This reflects their concept of self: a Ksar is a carried fragment of their ancestors and descendants. Dropping into singular ("I") is a sign of serious personal stake.

### The Vex

Four-armed, asymmetrical, with skin that shifts color in the presence of strong emotion (not theirs — other people's). The Vex are the galaxy's only confirmed survivors of the First One era — their lineage goes back ~90,000 years, and their cultural memory (oral, not written) preserves fragments of pre-Concordance galactic history. They are rare (fewer than 10,000 in known space), long-lived (~400 years), and regarded with reverence, fear, or hatred depending on the observer's faction.

**Mechanical bias:** +10% ability cooldown reduction. Locked out of the Verdant Church through-line (the Church considers Vex heretical by nature).

**Cultural notes:** Quiet, observant, unreadable. A Vex who takes an interest in a human's life becomes a fixture — a family cousin who was always there. A Vex who takes a dislike to a human becomes a patient, incomprehensible enemy.

**Speech pattern:** Short sentences. Precise nouns. Almost no adjectives; the Vex language has very few adjectives and the speaker treats that as a mark of discipline.

### Non-playable species (flavor)

- **The Drift-born** — humans born on ships, often in zero-g. Culturally distinct enough to be a subspecies. Considered for playable status at launch; moved to post-launch to control art scope.
- **The Menagerie's collected aliens** — the wandering circus-fleet collects exotic sapients. Several rare species exist only inside the Menagerie.
- **Husk-kind** — rumored survivors of the Unmaking who were "touched" by whatever happened. Mentioned in lore fragments; never confirmed.
- **The First Ones** — not a species the player meets. A presence, a geometry, a voice in ruins. Their actual form is a late-game revelation.

## 3. Geography

The explored galaxy is divided into **three great rings** around galactic center, plus the void between:

### The Core
The old heart of the Concordance. Contains roughly forty surviving inhabited systems, most dominated by the Iron Concord. Heavy infrastructure, expensive markets, tight patrols. Starting area for the **Gunslinger** and **Tactician** classes.

Notable systems:
- **Terra** — humanity's homeworld. Mostly quarantined after the Unmaking. Official Iron Concord capital is on the moon, Luna.
- **Proxima Hearth** — humanity's first colony. Now a trading hub.
- **Centauri Yards** — largest operational shipyard in the galaxy.

### The Frontier
Formerly Concordance expansion territory. Now a lawless mosaic of small states, pirate enclaves, corporate holdouts, and independent stations. This is where most of the game happens.

Notable systems:
- **Kepler Reach** — where the Kepler Silence happened. Haunted space. High-value salvage, high death rate.
- **The Bazaar** — a hollowed-out asteroid turned into the largest open market outside Iron Concord space. Owes allegiance to no one.
- **Hollow Sun** — a dead star whose system is the Hollow Fleet's only known staging ground. Never been successfully raided.

### The Deep Black
Beyond the Frontier. Mostly unexplored. What's out there is known only through rumor, Verdant Church revelation, and the occasional voidrunner who comes back wealthy, wrong in the head, or both. This is endgame content.

Notable:
- **The Lattice** — a stretch of space where First One ruin density is extraordinarily high. Most valuable and most lethal region in the game.
- **Nightshard** — a single system where time appears to run slower. Players notice the in-game clock drifting here.

## 4. Major factions

Six major factions. Each has its own ideology, aesthetic, starting disposition toward the player, and mission tree.

### 4.1 The Iron Concord

**Core belief:** The Concordance never really fell. It just needs restoring. We are its rightful successors.

**Reality:** The Concord is a bureaucratic military state holding a few dozen systems together with propaganda and patrol fleets. They are competent and dangerous but running on fumes. Their senior leadership is fractious and paranoid.

**Aesthetic:** Brass, steel grey, deep blue. Crisp uniforms. Clean lines. Ships that look like the Concordance navy — because they *are* the Concordance navy, a hundred years out of date and lovingly maintained.

**Disposition:** Neutral to players who don't antagonize them. Suspicious of voidrunners as a class. Will pay for First One artifacts but quietly.

**Hook:** High-paying legitimate missions, ship upgrades you can't get elsewhere, a questline that lets the player rise through the ranks and influence the Concord's direction.

### 4.2 The Free Worlds Congress

**Core belief:** The Concordance was a tyranny. Its fall was a gift. Never again.

**Reality:** A loose federation of trading colonies, frontier cities, and independent worlds with a shared parliament that barely functions. Militarily weaker than the Concord but economically dominant in the Frontier.

**Aesthetic:** White and sun-gold, rough-hewn materials, ships that are practical mashups of whatever parts were available. Less parade ground, more farmers' market.

**Disposition:** Friendly to voidrunners by default. Their economy depends on independents.

**Hook:** Trading missions, escort missions, political intrigue. The Congress has more internal drama than any other faction.

### 4.3 The Verdant Church

**Core belief:** The First Ones were gods. The Concordance angered them by disturbing their relics. The Unmaking was divine judgment. Humanity's only salvation is to abandon technology beyond a certain line — and to return every First One artifact to its resting place.

**Reality:** A religious movement with millions of adherents across the Frontier and Deep Black. Not monolithic — there are moderate Verdant who run charities and militant Verdant who execute voidrunners on sight. Run by a secretive **Theocrat Council**.

**Aesthetic:** Deep green, gold leaf, organic shapes. Ships that look like temples. Weapons that look ceremonial but hit like trucks.

**Disposition:** Hostile to voidrunners who loot First One ruins. Friendly to ones who turn in artifacts for reward (the artifacts go into Church vaults — players who want to be collectors won't like this).

**Hook:** A questline that asks the player what they believe. Verdant companions, Verdant-only items, a late-game revelation that the Church is at least partially *correct* about what the First Ones were.

### 4.4 The Hollow Fleet

**Core belief:** Nothing is spoken. Nothing is known.

**Reality:** A fleet of derelict Concordance warships, automated and hostile, that has been hunting in the galaxy for nearly fifty years. They don't communicate. They don't negotiate. They appear, engage, and withdraw. No captive has ever been taken; no officer of the fleet has ever been identified. Their crew — if they have one — has never been seen.

**Aesthetic:** Black, rust, silver. Running lights are all dead. Hull markings are scoured away. Their ships look like ghosts — because they are ghosts.

**Disposition:** Always hostile. Will pursue the player across multiple systems once aggroed.

**Hook:** The Hollow Fleet is the main antagonist. Figuring out what they are, where they come from, and how to stop them is a major questline. They are connected to the Unmaking.

### 4.5 The Scrapfather Syndicate

**Core belief:** Everything that exists was made by someone. Everything is salvage. Everything is for sale.

**Reality:** A criminal empire of scavengers, fences, smugglers, and pit-fighters run from The Bazaar. Ruled by a figure known only as the **Scrapfather**, whose identity is one of the game's recurring mysteries. Not a country — a distributed network of chop shops and black markets.

**Aesthetic:** Bright neon over rust and industrial mesh. Welded-together ships. Graffiti everywhere. Music in every station.

**Disposition:** Friendly to anyone with money. Hostile to cops (i.e., the Iron Concord). Amoral.

**Hook:** Black-market access, illegal ship modifications, pit fighting, heist missions, a questline about the Scrapfather's true identity.

### 4.6 The Homesteaders

**Core belief:** We were Concordance citizens once. Most of us still are. We just want to be left alone to rebuild.

**Reality:** Independent colonies and small alliances that have rejected both Iron Concord patronage and Free Worlds membership. Agricultural, craftsmanlike, fiercely protective of their communities. The galaxy's largest source of genuinely decent people. They don't call themselves "Homesteaders" — it's a voidrunner nickname that stuck. Among themselves, each colony just uses its own name.

**Aesthetic:** Warm wood, linen, practical gear. Small ships. Old Concordance technology kept running through five generations of maintenance.

**Disposition:** Neutral. Generous to voidrunners who help them, hostile to pirates and raiders.

**Hook:** Home-making missions, small-scale heroism, the most moving companion storylines in the game. The Homesteaders are who the player saves when they're being heroic.

*(Previously named "The Verdant Remnant" in draft 0.1 — renamed to avoid confusion with the Verdant Church.)*

## 4.7 Faction goals summary

Every faction runs a **long-term agenda** as AI-driven operations in the game. These goals are the engine of the sandbox — factions advance them with or without the player. The player can accelerate, oppose, or ignore each goal. See [`factions-and-quests.md`](systems/factions-and-quests.md) for the mechanical model.

| Faction | Primary goal | Posture | Ends the game if... |
|---|---|---|---|
| Iron Concord | Restore the Concordance order under Concord rule | Expansionist, suspicious, methodical | Player completes their through-line and raises the banner |
| Free Worlds Congress | Keep the frontier free; contain Concord expansion | Defensive, commercial, politically volatile | Player completes their through-line and reforms Congress |
| Verdant Church | Consecrate every First One site and end ruin-raiding | Zealous, factional, patient | Player completes their through-line and seals the sites |
| Hollow Fleet | Destroy all voidrunners disturbing First One sites | Hostile, silent, ancient | Player discovers their truth and chooses their role |
| Scrapfather Syndicate | Corner the galactic black market | Opportunist, amoral, extractive | Player learns the Scrapfather's identity |
| The Homesteaders | Protect and expand their fragile communities | Peaceful, communal, vulnerable | Player's protection let them found a new civilization |

**Crucial:** these goals are **running whether the player looks or not**. An ignored Verdant Church will slowly choke off voidrunner access to Kepler-7b. An ignored Hollow Fleet will quietly thin the voidrunner population. The galaxy changes on its own, and the player arrives at a different state every playthrough.

## 5. Minor factions (~10)

Brief sketches. Expand in follow-up passes.

1. **The Kepler Cartel** — voidrunner guild operating out of Kepler Reach. Specializes in high-risk salvage.
2. **House Aldebaran** — aristocratic remnants of pre-Concordance colonial nobility. Small territory, big ego.
3. **The Signal** — a rogue AI collective that broadcasts coded messages across the Frontier. Origin unknown.
4. **Red Harvest** — an insurrectionist militia based in former Iron Concord mining colonies. Anti-corporate, violent.
5. **The Menagerie** — a wandering circus-fleet that collects living alien specimens. Ethically... questionable.
6. **The Drift** — communities of ship-born humans who have never lived on a planet. Pacifist-leaning.
7. **The Archivists** — an academic order trying to preserve Concordance history. Poor, pedantic, occasionally heroic.
8. **Black Vein Syndicate** — a drug cartel. Produce and distribute a First One–derived narcotic called **nullbloom**.
9. **The Seventh Gate** — a cult that believes the Hollow Fleet is the answer rather than the enemy.
10. **The Longhand** — bounty hunters with a strict code. Will take a contract on the player; will never break one.

## 7. The central mystery (discoverable, not gated)

Black Star has a central mystery. Unlike a JRPG's main quest, it is **not a required path**. The mystery is a set of ~30 **discoverable revelations** scattered across the world in lore fragments, ruins, companion reveals, and rare static events. A player who engages with it unravels the story:

> **What caused the Unmaking, and is it going to happen again?**

The answers, in rough order of revelation difficulty:

1. The Unmaking was not random. There was a pattern to which worlds fell first.
2. The pattern correlates with the presence of First One ruins of a certain kind.
3. The First Ones did not vanish. They *hid themselves* inside the ruins, in a state that is neither life nor death. The Unmaking was their... *waking*. Partial, incomplete. Something interrupted it.
4. The Hollow Fleet is not a machine army. It is the *regret* of the dying Concordance navy, trying to hold the line against the next waking, and failing. They hunt voidrunners because voidrunners are the ones digging the First Ones back up.
5. The interrupted waking is starting again. The player's rumored Black Star artifact is a key to it.

### How the mystery is discovered

The player unlocks revelations by:

- **Reading lore fragments** in First One ruins (most common — every biome has ~5 fragments to find)
- **Recovering artifacts** and having a Voidsmith inspect them
- **Completing specific faction through-line stages** (Verdant Church, Hollow Fleet)
- **Triggering static set-piece events** — fixed locations in the galaxy where the player encounters a major revelation once per save
- **Companion loyalty reveals** — Dr. Lilit Veir and The Witness each unlock pieces

### Gating

- **≥15 revelations** unlocks a Codex entry labeled "The Truth" that summarizes the full story.
- **≥20 revelations** unlocks **endgame access** — a final Lattice coordinate and the endgame encounter.
- A player with fewer than 15 revelations never learns the full truth, and never unlocks the endgame encounter. They can still end the game through other paths (faction through-line, Warlord independence, Wanderer retirement, Homesteader protection).

### The endings and the mystery

- **Keeper** — requires ≥15 revelations + Verdant Church through-line. Seal the First One sites forever, at the cost of the galaxy's greatest treasures.
- **Destroyer** (secret) — requires ≥20 revelations + Hollow Fleet through-line. Wake the First Ones yourself.
- **Restorer / Free World / Warlord / Wanderer / Homesteader** — can be reached without engaging the mystery at all. The truth is never forced on the player.

The core design principle: **the mystery is the galaxy's weather**. You can watch it, study it, try to stop it, or ignore it and live your own life. Black Star respects all four.

## 8. Writing style guide

Every line of dialogue and lore fragment in Black Star should pass these filters:

### Voice
- **Terse over florid.** Space is cold. People who live here don't waste words. A single "Yeah." can do more work than a paragraph.
- **Specific over general.** Not "a long time ago" — "eleven jumps from here, the week before my sister died, I saw one of these."
- **Grounded over grand.** Avoid "destiny," "chosen one," "ancient prophecy." Use "job," "contract," "rumor."
- **Dry humor is encouraged.** The galaxy is grim. Characters cope. Let them.

### Dialogue
- People swear, but sparingly. Overuse kills impact.
- Characters don't explain their backstories unprompted. The player earns backstories through loyalty.
- Accents and dialects are OK but must be consistent and readable. No phonetic spelling.
- Every named character has a verbal tic or speech pattern distinguishable from every other. Sable says "aye" where others say "yes." Runt curses constantly. Thane never uses contractions.

### Lore fragments
- Fragments are found in ruins (data slates, captain's logs, graffiti, broken holograms)
- Each fragment should do at least two of:
  1. Give a concrete fact about the world
  2. Imply a much bigger truth
  3. Tell a small human story
  4. Leave the reader with a question
- Fragments should never over-explain. The mystery stays mysterious.

### What we don't write
- No ironic/snarky Joss Whedon quippery. That's a different tone.
- No exposition dumps. Every fact must be earned.
- No cartoon villains. Even the Hollow Fleet has a tragic explanation.
- No fourth-wall breaks. The world takes itself seriously.

## 9. Themes

The three thematic threads the narrative returns to:

1. **What do you rebuild after the end of the world?** — the central question every faction answers differently, and every player answers for themselves.
2. **The past is not your friend.** — the Concordance the Iron Concord wants to restore was never as good as they remember. The First Ones the Verdant worship are not gods. Nostalgia is dangerous.
3. **You are not alone, even out here.** — the game is about voidrunners (loners by profession) slowly building crews, families, and empires. The core emotional arc is from isolation to belonging.
